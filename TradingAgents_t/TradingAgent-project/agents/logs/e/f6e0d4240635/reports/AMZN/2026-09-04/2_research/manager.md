**Recommendation**: Overweight

**Rationale**: This debate came down to a narrow question: does the bear have a demand problem, a valuation problem, or just a distaste for the price? On the operating business, the bear capitulated — and that matters, because it was the bear's own stated burden. AMZN grew revenue +19.6% in Q2 (accelerating from +16.6% in Q1), grew operating income +43.2% to $27.5B, expanded operating margin 230bp to 13.7%, grew TTM operating cash flow +33.2% to $161.4B, and did it with inventory flat at $38.2B, marketing +2.5% against +19.6% revenue, and SBC down to 3.0% of revenue. The bull asked for AWS share loss, inventory build, contract-liability decline, or margin rollover; none were produced, and the margin data is the decisive item — margins expanded *while* D&A was already running +31% y/y and capex was at peak intensity. That is operating leverage outrunning depreciation, and it is the single hardest fact in the debate to explain away.

The bear's strongest ground was capital intensity and the balance-sheet trajectory, and it deserves real weight: capex went $44.2B → $54.2B sequentially (not flattening, which directly punctures the bull's "if capex merely flattens, FCF inflects" conditional), capex is 27% of revenue and 2.3x TTM D&A, gross debt rose 92% to $132.2B in six months, interest expense rose 155% and flipped to net interest expense, and the quick ratio is 0.875. But the bear overplayed level as if it were distress: AMZN still holds +$30.4B net cash ex-operating leases, net debt including leases is under five months of operating cash flow, and Q2 interest of $1.314B is roughly 5% of $27.5B of operating income. The bear's "pre-sold demand" rebuttal also cuts both ways — flat contract liabilities at ~$24.9B does undercut the bull's backlog framing, but AMZN has never disclosed large RPO the way Microsoft does, so flat contract liabilities is not evidence of weak demand. And the bear's decision to lump $650B of contractual obligations in with debt is a conflation; a large share is power and capacity that scales with revenue, not fixed financial leverage.

The bear's valuation argument is its weakest. Its 32x core TTM EPS claim is arithmetically fair, but it prices 32x against core EPS growing ~29% — roughly a 1.1x PEG on an accelerating, margin-expanding franchise. That is a full multiple, not an indefensible one, and the bear built its case on "priced for perfection" without a peer or historical anchor to support it. The earnings-quality risk is real but already disclosed and largely understood: the $62.8B H1 mark-up inflates reported EPS to $5.75 versus ~$2.16 core, and the Q2 2027 like-for-like air pocket is a genuine headline risk — but the bull's answer, that core EPS still grew 29% ex-mark-up, is the correct way to underwrite it.

The tape is where the bear genuinely scores, and where both sides actually agree: AMZN at $258.51 sits below its 10-EMA (259.14), VWMA (259.78), and 20-SMA, with a resistance stack at 259–262.20, lower highs at 287.20 → 278.84 → 267.56, MACD line only 0.339 above zero, and RSI at 49.55. The bull's own plan says don't chase and wait for 262.20 confirmation. That is not a bear thesis, it is an entry-timing observation — the primary trend is still intact with price 8.11% above a rising 200-SMA at 239.12 and a widening golden-cross spread. But it is enough to stop me short of a full Buy at a mid-range price against an overhead wall.

So: the bull won the operating and valuation debates, the bear won the sequencing-and-entry debate, and the balance sheet is a trajectory concern rather than a solvency one. That nets to constructive but staged — Overweight, not Buy. The known gap in the evidence is explicit: AWS segment revenue and margin cannot be verified from what was provided, and there is no Q3 guidance or capex plan in this record. Those two absences are precisely why I want the position built in tranches on confirmation rather than taken in one clip.
