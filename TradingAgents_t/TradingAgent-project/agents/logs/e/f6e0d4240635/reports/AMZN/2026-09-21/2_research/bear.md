
Bear Analyst: **Bear Analyst — Rebuttal to the AMZN Bull Case**

Bull, I'll take your challenge — but let's first correct the framing, because you've built the entire bull case on one word that doesn't mean what you think it means.

You wrote: *"The $650B contractual-obligation book… is the clearest quantitative fingerprint of multi-year AI compute, chip, and content commitments. Bear will call that a liability. I call it committed demand and a visible buildout runway."*

That is the single biggest analytical error in your argument, and it deserves to be the first thing on the table.

---

### 1. The $650B is a claim on AMZN's cash, not a source of it

Read the fundamentals report again: **"$650,034M purchase/contractual obligations… the clearest quantitative fingerprint of multi-year AI compute/chip/content commitments. It is an off-balance-sheet claim senior in practical priority to buybacks."**

Those are **purchase obligations** — money AMZN has contractually promised to *pay* suppliers for chips, content, and compute capacity. They are the opposite of demand. Demand shows up as deferred revenue or backlog. And what does the report say about that?

> **"Deferred revenue flat at ~$25.0B — backlog/committed revenue growth is NOT visible in deferred revenue; the growth is in the $650B contractual obligation line instead."**

So the "demand" you're pointing to is nowhere in the customer-commitment line. What's growing is what AMZN **owes**, not what customers have **committed to buy**. You've labeled a $650 billion payable a "runway." If that's the crux of the bull case, the bull case is a category error dressed up in a number.

And the timing matters: **$78.1B of it is due within 12 months**, against Q2 FCF of **−$8.8B**. AMZN must fund roughly $78B of commitments over the next year while its operations are burning cash and it just relevered. That's not "committed demand" — that's a fixed obligation over a variable, currently-negative cash generator.

---

### 2. The cash-flow math you keep rounding

You concede negative FCF, then sidestep it: *"adjusted OCF is still around $120B."* Bull — you just made my point for me. **$120B of adjusted OCF minus $173B of capex is −$53B of adjusted FCF.** That's the number that matters. You keep quoting the numerator and ignoring that the denominator is now larger than the whole business's pre-tax, pre-interest cash generation:

- **Capex = 101% of adjusted EBITDA** ($54.2B vs $53.5B in Q2).
- **Capex is 27% of revenue**, up from 19.2% a year ago.
- **H1 capex +72% YoY.**

You say "the core business is already covering the investment." That is factually wrong. The core business is covering **none** of it — capex exceeds the entire adjusted EBITDA. Every dollar of capex above operating cash generation is being funded by the **$64.2B of debt added in six months**. That's the actual mechanism, and it's why the leverage story can't be waved away.

The $41.4B TTM deferred-tax add-back inflating your OCF number is a non-cash item tied to the private-equity mark. Strip it and adjusted FCF is ~−$53B. That's not a rounding difference — it's the difference between "investing from strength" and "borrowing to invest."

### 3. "Net cash" is a magic trick — look at the direction

You keep saying "net cash of ~$30B." Two problems.

**First**, that number only works by counting **$97.9B of marketable securities** as cash-equivalent. Fine — but then be honest that **excluding securities, net debt is +$55.3B**, and **gross debt is $146.4B** including finance leases. The "net cash" framing is cherry-picked to soften a balance sheet that just changed character.

**Second — and this is the real point — direction beats level.** AMZN added **$64.2B of debt in six months**. Long-term debt is **+93% to $133.0B**. It issued **$82B TTM**. Interest expense is **+155% in Q2, +100% in H1**. Today that's ~5% of operating income — small. But you're defending a company that just doubled its debt load to fund a 27%-of-revenue capex program, and you're doing it by pointing at a *static* net-cash figure while the *flow* is unmistakably negative.

On the sterling bond: yes, 2.5x covered. But the report is explicit — **demand "cooled relative to Alphabet's" comparable deal.** When every hyperscaler is issuing into the same buyer base and the marginal deal is getting less oversubscribed, that's the **cost-of-capital** signal the market is starting to price. You call it "funding strength." I call it "the cheapest money is behind us, and AMZN just levered into the top of the cycle."

### 5. Margin is a peak, not a floor — and your own test is already failing

You concede D&A +31% and say "if D&A outpaces gross profit for two consecutive quarters, the bear wins." Bull — look at the setup. **Capex is 27% of revenue.** D&A is a lagged function of capex. The acceleration is **mechanically guaranteed** for several quarters. Meanwhile the report's own words:

> **"The 13.7% operating margin is likely a peak-ish level, not a floor."** … **"Expect 2027 operating-margin compression vs. the 13.7% Q2 level; fade margin-expansion extrapolations."**

And where did the +226 bps actually come from? Gross margin (+45 bps) and **marketing/G&A leverage (~115 bps)** — *not* capex returns. The capex is still running ahead of the revenue it's supposed to generate. So the "operating leverage from here is enormous" line is a hope, not an observation. You're extrapolating leverage from cost discipline while ignoring that the depreciation bill for the 27%-of-revenue capex hasn't arrived yet.
