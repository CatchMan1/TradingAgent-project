
Bear Analyst: # BEAR CASE: AMZN — The Bull Found One Good Quarter and Built a Cathedral on It

Let me start by giving the bull full credit for the strongest part of their case, because I intend to take it apart precisely where it's strongest — not where it's weakest.

They're right that the Q2 operating line is genuinely good. Revenue +19.6%, operating income +43.2%, margin +225 bps, G&A down, SBC down, marketing up only 3.9%. That is real operating leverage. I'm not going to pretend otherwise, and any bear who does is selling you something.

But here's the problem with everything the bull built on top of it: **the market has already read that 10-Q, and it has spent the last eight sessions voting against the price the bull wants to buy.** AMZN is down 6.65% from the 8/3 close, has printed four consecutive lower lows, and closed the last session *below* the 38.2% retracement the bull keeps citing as "support" (265.48 versus a 265.13 close). The bull calls that a "coil." I call it what it is: the market trimming an overreaction, one lower high at a time.

So let me take the bull's arguments in the order they made them, and show you where the math stops working.

---

## 1. The "filing-driven repricing" is a narrative, not a fact

The bull's cleanest rhetorical move is this: *"The gap wasn't a mystery — the 10-Q was accepted 7/31. The market got informed, and information is permanent."*

Let me test that. The 7/30 close was 235.50 on **101.8M shares** — already 2.5x normal volume *before* the gap. The 7/31 session *gapped open at 265.00*, a **+12.51% opening print**. That tells you the information was already out — an earnings release or call — before the formal 10-Q was accepted. The 10-Q is the *footnote document*, not the catalyst. The bull is dressing up a press-release reaction in the language of "audited-quality disclosure" to make it sound permanent. It isn't.

And "permanent information" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Permanent information can still be **overpriced in the first 48 hours**. A +20.6% two-session move on a $2.5T company is one of the largest single-event re-ratings in mega-cap history. The market has now had eight sessions to decide whether 13.7% margins deserve to be capitalized at that level — and it is steadily deciding **no**.

Ask yourself the bull's own question honestly: if this was pure "informed repricing," why is the stock making lower highs *and* lower lows every single session since 8/3? Informed markets don't usually spend two weeks unwinding their own epiphany.

## 2. The "core EPS" math the bull gave you actually makes AMZN expensive

This is the tell. The bull concedes — correctly — that $50.5B of Q2's $62.6B net income is a non-cash equity mark, and that core EPS is ~$1.95, not $5.75. Then they say, essentially, "see, still fine."

- Core EPS $1.95 × 4 = **~$7.80 annualized**

- With **negative TTM free cash flow of −$11.6B**

The bull's own corrected number puts AMZN at 34x earnings *with a cash burn*. That is not "not demanding" — that's a growth multiple being asked to absorb a capex supercycle whose returns are unproven. The bull's valuation section conveniently switches to **3.6x sales** and never returns to the core-earnings multiple their own analysis produced. That's not a coincidence. 34x core with negative FCF is a much harder sell than 3.6x sales with "accelerating growth."

And the tax point cuts against them too. The bull says "hold the old 12.8% rate and core EPS would be $2.20." But the rate is **~25% going forward** — the fundamental report explicitly calls it "a structural headwind to model for the rest of the year, not a one-off distortion." The bull is using an *obsolete* tax rate to argue the real number is understated. That's not conservatism. That's a hypothetical.

## 3. The bull's best argument is actually my best argument — they just read it backwards

Here's where I want to be surgical, because the bull planted their flag on this and it's the wrong hill.

**The bull says:** "Operating income grew 43% while D&A grew 32%. That crossover kills the bear case. Show me the quarter where D&A outruns operating income."

**My answer: you're celebrating the accounting lag, not the economics.**

Look at the actual ratio: **TTM capex $173.0B against TTM D&A of $75.2B — a 2.3x gap.** That $98B/year of capex that has been spent but *not yet depreciated*. D&A is a lagging indicator by construction; it hits the P&L over the asset life. The bull is pointing at the fact that the depreciation from the current buildout **hasn't arrived yet** and calling it proof the buildout is profitable.

That's not a crossover. That's the calm before the crossover.

If capex holds anywhere near $54B/quarter, D&A must accelerate materially for years just to converge toward the capital base being deployed. D&A is already +32% YoY while revenue is +19.6%. The margin expansion the bull is underwriting is being delivered *partly because* the depreciation bill is deferred. When it lands — and the report flags "D&A ramp has years left to run" explicitly — the 225 bps of margin expansion becomes the first thing that compresses.

And notice what's actually driving the margin beat: **G&A −3.9%, SBC −1.5%, marketing +3.9%.** Those are cost-discipline levers. They're real, but they are also *finite*. You cannot cut G&A forever. The bull is extrapolating a one-quarter cost-discipline flex and a deferred depreciation bill into a multi-year margin story. That's the weakest kind of extrapolation.

## 4. Negative FCF, and the "0.41x leverage" sleight of hand

The bull's leverage answer is 0.41x net debt / OCF. Clean number. Let me show you what it hides.

- Net debt went from **~$37.8B to ~$66.7B in six months — +76%.**
- Long-term debt **+93%** ($68.8B → $133.0B), $81.9B issued TTM vs $0.7B a year ago.
- Interest expense **+155% in Q2 alone.**
- **TTM FCF is −$11.6B**, a ~$25B swing from +$13.5B.
- H1 FCF: **−$27.0B.**

The bull says "the shortfall is only 7% of the $161B cash engine." True *this quarter*. But you cannot service debt out of operating income that is being fully consumed by capex. Interest is paid in cash. The company is borrowing **$82B a year** to fund a buildout whose returns are exactly what is unproven. 0.41x is a *snapshot* of a ratio that is moving in the wrong direction at 76% per six months.

And the ratio itself understates the obligation. It excludes **$650B of total contractual obligations, $78.1B due within 12 months**, plus $96.3B of operating lease liabilities only partly visible in the net-debt line. The bull's "five months of OCF covers net debt" is a parlor trick — it ignores that the OCF is already spoken for by the capex that created the debt.

**Here's my number, since the bull asked for one:** net debt + lease obligations + purchase commitments against *free* cash flow. Free cash flow is negative. That's the metric. On a cash-flow basis, AMZN is currently funding growth entirely from the balance sheet, and the market has historically been unforgiving about that when growth wobbles — 2022 is the exhibit. AMZN fell roughly **50% peak-to-trough** in that de-rating. The trigger wasn't insolvency; it was the market losing patience with capex ROI. That's the risk the bull's "0.41x" is designed to make you forget.

## 5. The receivables point — the bull leaned on a mischaracterization

The bull writes: *"Allowance rose proportionately ($2.4B → $2.8B), which is reassuring."*

Run the numbers. AR grew **+30.1%** ($67.7B → $88.1B). The allowance grew **+16.7%** ($2.4B → $2.8B). That is **not proportionate** — the allowance grew at roughly **55% of the AR growth rate**. Allowance coverage *declined* from ~3.5% to ~3.2% of net receivables.

The fundamental report calls that "proportionate." The arithmetic says otherwise. A 12-point gap between receivables growth and revenue growth, paired with *declining* reserve coverage, is precisely the kind of divergence that precedes a collections discussion. The bull cited the reassurance without checking the math. That's the whole debate in miniature: the bull is reading the qualitative line and skipping the ratio.

## 6. The moat evidence is a footnote dressed as a thesis

**Bedrock/OpenAI:** Two cybersecurity models landing on Bedrock is a *reseller* relationship. It demonstrates that AMZN is a **marketplace for other companies' frontier models** — not that it owns one. Google has Gemini. Microsoft has its OpenAI relationship. Meta has Llama. AMZN's differentiated position in the model layer is that it is *dependent* on third parties. That's not a moat widening; it's a moat being rented. And one model launch is, by the bull's own admission, immaterial to revenue.

**The capex-funding screen:** The report flags this as "a paraphrased TV/segment summary, not a research note with numbers," and labels it "second-order and soft." The bull leans on it anyway, because the hard data doesn't say what they want.

**The $1.15T 2027 AI capex projection:** The bull calls it a tailwind. It's also a **supply** signal. If the entire industry deploys $1.15T into AI infrastructure with uncertain demand, you get overcapacity and commoditization — the same dynamic that has crushed every prior infrastructure buildout cycle. AMZN's own capex is **27% of revenue**. At that intensity, the risk isn't "AMZN misses the boom" — it's "AMZN overbuilds into a boom that normalizes."

**Zoox:** A *fleetwide software recall after a smoke-related safety incident* is a regulatory and reputational datapoint for a program the bull wants credit for. It's immaterial to the P&L, yes — which is precisely why it shouldn't be in the bull case at all.

## 8. The deferred-tax argument is double-counted

The bull finishes with: *"H1 deferred tax was $30.5B, TTM $41.4B. That reverses and flatters future GAAP EPS."*

Watch the logic carefully. The deferred tax liability exists **because of the mark-up**. If the mark-up reverses (a markdown), the deferred tax reverses as a *benefit* — but the markdown itself hits pre-tax income as a **loss**. Run it: a 30% markdown of the $122.3B book is a ~$37B pre-tax hit; at a ~25% rate, the tax benefit is ~$9B, for a **net ~$28B GAAP loss**. The bull wants you to book the tax benefit *without* the pre-tax charge. You can't have both. Either the mark holds (no tax reversal) or it reverses (big loss). The bull is picking the favorable half of a two-sided entry.

And on the "embedded value" itself: a non-marketable equity book that went from **$16.2B to $122.3B in six months — a 655% increase, with $50.5B marked up in a single quarter** — is not "validated value." It is a **valuation-reliability risk**. These are VC-style marks that can be stale or driven by a single funding round. The bull treats them as hard assets and then, in the same breath, tells you a markdown would be a "buying opportunity." That's an admission the marks aren't reliable — which is exactly my point.
