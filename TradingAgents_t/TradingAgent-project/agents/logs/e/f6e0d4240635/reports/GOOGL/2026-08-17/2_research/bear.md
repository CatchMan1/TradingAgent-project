## Opening: The Bull Is Reading the Same Chart — He's Just Reading It Backwards

Let me thank my colleague for doing half my job for me. He conceded the tape is bad. He conceded the headline EPS is fake. He conceded the depreciation cliff is real. He conceded there's a **$32 billion unexplained outflow**. Then he asked you to buy at $344 anyway — and called it a "747."

Here's the problem with the 747 metaphor: **the plane is fine. The fuel gauge isn't.** Alphabet's operating engine is genuinely excellent. But the equity is being asked to carry a $4.2 trillion valuation on near-zero free cash flow, 78% non-cash earnings, a depreciation wave that hasn't landed, and a tape making lower highs into a falling 50-day. Let me take his case apart point by point.

## 1. The Smoking Gun: The Margin Trend Has Already Rolled Over

The bull's entire fundamental case rests on **YoY** margin expansion — gross +215bp, operating +160bp. He says this "refutes" the capex bear case.

Now look at the **sequential** data, which is the most recent information in the file:

| Metric | Q1 2026 | Q2 2026 | QoQ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $109,896M | $119,796M | +9.0% |
| Gross margin | 62.4% | 61.7% | **−70bp** |
| Operating margin | 36.1% | 34.0% | **−210bp** |
| Cost of revenue | $41,271M | $45,943M | **+11.3%** |
| G&A | $4,291M | $6,461M | **+50.6%** |
| Operating income | $39,696M | $40,770M | **+2.7%** |

Operating income grew **2.7% sequentially** on revenue growth of **9.0%**. That's the definition of negative operating leverage — right now, in the most recent quarter, during the capex ramp the bull says is "already earning its cost of capital."

He's quoting the YoY column because it's flattering against a weak Q2 2025 base. The QoQ column is where the business is actually heading. Cost of revenue outgrew revenue sequentially. G&A jumped 50% — the report doesn't even tell us why, though the $1.76B rise in the loss contingency is a candidate. That's the capex and infrastructure cost biting *today*, not "in 2027."

And the depreciation wave the bull concedes is "real and coming" hasn't arrived. H1 depreciation $13.6B (+43% YoY) on $80.6B of H1 capex and **$29.1B incurred but unpaid**. If capex runs at $170B/year and depreciates over ~6 years, steady-state depreciation roughly doubles to $50B+ within a few years. The bull calls that "manageable arithmetic." It's manageable *only if 24% revenue growth persists indefinitely.* That's not the base case for a company this size. It's the bull case.

## 2. FCF: The Bull Wants You to Ignore the Cash Flow Statement

The bull's defense is that "OCF is booming, only the investment line moved." That **is** the problem. The investment line is now $80.6B per half and accelerating.

- **Q2 FCF: −$5.9B.**
- **H1 FCF: $4.3B** — an annualized FCF yield of roughly **0.3%** on a $4.2T market cap.
- **Capex/revenue: 37.5% in Q2.**
- **$29.1B of capex incurred but unpaid** — a forward indicator that Q3 capex likely exceeds $45B, and Q3 FCF is likely negative again.

The bull says "management chose to invest." Fine. But you don't get to value a stock on OCF and ignore the capex that consumes it. That's a restaurant boasting about food sales while ignoring food costs. FCF is what's left for shareholders. Right now, there's almost nothing left.

The bull also says buybacks going to zero is "a feature, not a bug." It's a feature if the capex earns its return. It's a bug if it doesn't — and we won't know until 2027–2028. Meanwhile, the **corporate bid that supported the stock for a decade is gone**, and the share count **rose 1.17%** (plus $19B of convertible preferred senior to common). The bull waves this away as "1.17% vs 24% revenue growth." But revenue per share growth isn't the same as EPS growth when the buyback tailwind is removed and interest expense is up 390%.

## 3. "Core EPS" at 32x Is Neither Cheap Nor Clean

The bull says: "Ignore the $14.24, use my $10.60 annualized core EPS, and 32x is fine."

Let me take that seriously. $10.60 is an **estimate** — the report itself derives it. It includes ~$5.6B annualized of interest income (which falls as cash is spent on capex) and ~$3.6B annualized of interest expense (which is rising 390% YoY as debt doubles). The net interest tailwind is already reversing. The "core" number is generous, not conservative.

And 32x for a company with **negative Q2 FCF**, a rising share count, a depreciation cliff, and 78% of GAAP earnings from non-cash marks? The S&P 500 trades at roughly 20x forward earnings. The bull calls GOOGL "compressed." I call it a premium multiple on deteriorating cash-flow quality.

His EV/EBIT point is instructive. Market cap $4.2T minus net cash ~$139B = EV ~$4.07T. Annualized EBIT ~$163B. That's **~25x EV/EBIT** — a premium to the market, for a business about to absorb a massive depreciation step-up. That's not a bargain. That's a full price for a highly uncertain return on $170B/year of capex.

## 4. The "Bull Flag" Is a Descending Triangle

The bull calls the setup "a bull flag above a rising 200 SMA on contracting volume with a higher low." Let me translate:

- The highs are **357.61 → 350.45 → 347.25**. That's a decisively lower-high sequence.
- A flat support with lower highs is a **descending triangle**, not a bull flag. Those break down more often than not.
- The **50 SMA has fallen every session since July 13** (372.80 → 353.33, −5.2%). The **10 EMA has been below the 50 SMA since late July**. The **MACD histogram is negative and widening**.

The bull says the MACD cross is a "lower-quality failed-rally cross." Correct — and failed-rally crosses *below* the zero line typically precede continuation lower, not reversal. He's citing the bearish interpretation as if it's bullish.

On volume: he says "distribution without volume is drift." But volume contraction into a decline also means **buyers have stepped away**. Healthy accumulation shows volume drying up on pullbacks and expanding on rallies. Here, we saw heavy volume on the July 23 gap down (69.4M) and the August 5 distribution day (46.9M), and light volume on the drift. That's the opposite of accumulation.

And the bull's "the stock refuses to get oversold" line? RSI hit **31.02** on July 23 — one point from oversold. That's not refusing; that's a near-miss. Now at 46, there's room to fall before any real support emerges.

## 6. Berkshire Is a Lagged Disclosure, Not a Catalyst

The bull's headline flow argument is Berkshire's $17B add. Three problems:

1. **It's as of June 30 — six and a half weeks stale.** Berkshire could have trimmed in July or August. We have no idea.
2. **The "$357 cost basis" is a rough blend.** The report explicitly says "treat only as a rough sanity check." The $37.9B ÷ 106M calculation blends Class A, Class C, and prior holdings bought at much lower prices. The new $17B could have been bought anywhere from $315 to $408 during Q2. We don't know.
3. **Berkshire being underwater isn't bullish.** The bull says "that buyer doesn't panic, that buyer adds." Maybe. Or the stock is below where the smartest long-term buyer got in — which is a warning, not a comfort. Berkshire bought Apple in 2016 and was underwater within months. The stock didn't care. Value investors are early; that's the job.

Meanwhile, the bull dismisses Pershing Square and Viking exits as "one manager's call, six weeks stale." By his own logic, the 13F data cuts both ways. He can't have it both ways — Berkshire's add is a catalyst, but Ackman's exit is noise. That's inconsistent.

## 7. Waymo Is an Expensive Option, Not a Catalyst

The bull calls Waymo "unpriced catalyst." Precisely:

- The news report itself says Waymo revenue is **"not material to Alphabet's near-term P&L."**
- Regulatory approval ≠ launch. The report flags **"permit scope, timing and conditions are unverified."**
- San Francisco was already served. "Adding" may include expanded service areas, not net-new markets.
- **Waymo loses money and will for years.** It's a cash sink embedded in Other Bets, not a cash generator.

Options cost money. Alphabet is spending billions on Waymo, and the payoff is years away. The bull says "you're getting it for free." You're not — you're paying for it through the capex line and the operating losses. The market has had **eight years** to price Waymo's option value. One permit expansion in California isn't going to re-rate a $4.2 trillion company.

## 8. The $32 Billion Elephant and the Dilution

The fundamental report flags **~$32B of investing outflow** not explained by capex, marketable securities, or other investments — most plausibly an acquisition, consistent with the **329% jump in finite-lived intangibles** and **$30.5B of common stock issued**.

The bull says: "If it's a strategic acquisition funded with stock at 1.17% dilution, that's buying growth." **He doesn't know what they bought. Neither do I.** That's the point.

Alphabet has a mixed M&A history — Motorola, Nest, and others have been value-destructive. A $32B acquisition with no disclosure in the evidence set is a material unknown. The bull treats it as a non-event. I treat it as a risk that needs verification before paying 32x earnings.

And the **first-ever $19B convertible preferred issuance** is a signal. Why raise senior capital when you have $242B of cash? Because cash is now precious for capex. The bull calls it a "rounding error." It's not — it's senior to common, it pays a dividend, and it converts into dilution.
