
Bear Analyst: **Bear Analyst: GOOGL — the operating business is good; the stock is not a good risk/reward here.**

Bull, I’ll concede the strongest part of your case up front: **GOOGL’s core operating momentum is real.** H1 revenue +23.0% to $229.7B, operating income +30.0%, operating margin 35.0%, and core EPS up ~21.6% are genuinely impressive. I’m not going to pretend this is a broken franchise.

But that is exactly where your argument slips. You’re using a strong operating headline to underwrite a **$4.2T market-cap stock** that is already discounting a lot of good news, while the cash-flow line, capital-allocation regime, technical tape, and near-term catalyst calendar are all deteriorating. My bear case is not “GOOGL is a bad company.” It’s “GOOGL is a poor risk/reward at $343.92 going into a Q3 print that could show negative free cash flow and a mechanical death cross.”

### 1. “Core EPS grew 22%” is true — but the valuation and future cost wave matter

You frame 31–32x core EPS as “reasonable.” Let’s put real numbers on it. At $343.92 and 12.23B shares, GOOGL’s market cap is roughly **$4.2T**. H1 core EPS was ~$5.29, so annualized core EPS is roughly **$10.6–$11.0**, or about **31–32x core earnings**.

That is not cheap for a company whose **H1 free cash flow collapsed to $4.3B from $24.3B** and whose accrual-basis FCF was roughly **–$14B**. Annualizing reported H1 FCF gives about $8.5B against a $4.2T market cap — a **0.2% FCF yield**. That is the number that matters if capex stays elevated.

And the cost wave is not hypothetical. H1 capex was **$80.6B**, up 103%, while depreciation was only **$13.6B**, up 43%. Capex is now **35.1% of revenue** versus D&A at **5.9%**. That gap guarantees a multi-year depreciation headwind. Add SBC up 32%, interest expense up 514%, and an effective tax rate that has drifted from 13.9% to 19.1%, and “core EPS up 22%” starts to look like the high-water mark, not the run-rate. The bull case treats capex as a moat; I see a cost base being built before the returns are proven.

### 2. The capex story is a competitive arms race, not a proven moat

Bull, you say GOOGL is “choosing to accelerate a land-grab.” Fine. But a moat protects returns. An AI capex arms race can destroy returns if every major player is forced to spend at the same time — Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, OpenAI. GOOGL is not spending $80.6B because it has a unique, high-return project with visible cash payback; it’s spending because it cannot afford to fall behind in AI infrastructure, cloud capacity, and distribution defense.

That distinction matters. If AI demand monetizes slower than expected, or pricing pressure intensifies, GOOGL will have locked in the depreciation and interest costs before proving the revenue. Q3 is the next test: the fundamentals report flags **$29.1B of accrued but unpaid capex** rolling into the period and says Q3 cash capex could exceed **$45B**. Negative FCF is a plausible outcome. You call that a buying opportunity if revenue and margins hold. I call it a **quality-of-earnings catalyst** — especially when the stock is already at 32x core EPS.

### 3. The balance sheet is a cushion, not a thesis

Yes, GOOGL has **$242.5B of cash and short-term investments** against **$101.1B of debt** — roughly **$141B net cash**, current ratio ~2.8x. Solvency is not the debate. But $141B net cash is only about 3% of a $4.2T market cap. It does not protect the multiple if FCF turns negative and capex guidance rises again.

Meanwhile, the capital-allocation regime has changed in a way bulls are underestimating. H1 2026: **buybacks were zero** versus $28.3B in H1 2025; GOOGL raised roughly **$109B** externally, including $30.5B of common stock and $19.1B of convertible preferred; shares outstanding rose **1.2%**; interest expense is now material relative to collapsing FCF; and the deferred tax liability jumped to **$22.8B** from $0.9B. That is not a shareholder-return story right now. It is a “fund the buildout, dilute a little, and hope the capex peak arrives soon” story. The bull says buyback resumption is a ready catalyst — but there is no timetable, and Q3 could show the opposite: more spending.

### 4. The “optionality” is not free at this valuation

You point to Anthropic, Waymo, and space-based AI as free options. They are not free. At a $4.2T market cap, even the reported **$124B Anthropic stake** is only about **3%** of the company’s value — and that figure is “reportedly” and “on paper,” private and illiquid. Waymo has real long-term potential, but the news flow is long-dated: Tokyo is a **2027** plan; Las Vegas headlines conflict on whether Waymo is “planning to enter” or already offering public rides; Texas fleet growth of 49% is off an undisclosed base. Space-based AI is experimental and financially negligible near term.

You’re not getting these options for free. You’re paying 32x core EPS and a 0.2% FCF yield for them, while the near-term news flow includes macro yield pressure explicitly naming GOOGL, EU DMA Search-compliance overhang, and AI-competition headlines from OpenAI and Meta. That is not a setup where optionality carries the stock. It’s a setup where optionality gets discounted until the cash flow stabilizes.

### Bear bottom line

GOOGL is a strong operating franchise. That is not the debate. The debate is whether investors are being paid for the risks at **$343.92**. I don’t think they are.

You’ve got a stock at roughly **32x core EPS**, a **0.2% FCF yield**, a capex super-cycle that guarantees higher depreciation and interest, zero buybacks, rising share count, a $22.8B deferred-tax overhang, a $17.4B loss-contingency accrual, a negative near-term news mix, and a tape making lower highs with weak bounce volume. The bull case requires the market to look through a negative FCF print, ignore a death cross, and pay for long-dated optionality before the capex returns are visible. That is a lot to ask.

**My recommendation: avoid/underweight GOOGL at current levels.** Do not initiate a new long in the $342–$345 cluster. Existing holders should use rallies into **$350–$355** to trim or hedge. Until then, the risk is that the market stops paying for the story and starts pricing the cash flow.
