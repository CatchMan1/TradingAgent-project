
Bear Analyst: **Bear Analyst Rebuttal: GOOGL — The Bull Is Paying 33x Core Earnings for Negative Free Cash Flow**

Bull, I’ll give you the revenue line. It’s genuinely strong. Q2 FY2026 revenue growth of **24.2%** is real, and **30.4% operating income growth** is real. But you’re making a classic mistake: you’re underwriting an operating-income story while asking investors to own a free-cash-flow story. At **$343.50**, GOOGL is not cheap on cash, the technicals are not bullish, and your catalysts are either long-dated or hope. Let me take your points one by one.

## 1. “It’s an investment cycle, not a liquidity crisis”

Agreed. It’s not a solvency issue. But that’s not the bear case. The bear case is that **GOOGL’s equity story has shifted from capital return to capital consumption**, and the market is repricing that.

Look at the actual cash math:

- **Q2 FY2026 free cash flow: −$5.9B** — the first negative quarter in the dataset.
- **1H FCF: $4.3B vs. $24.3B** a year ago — down **82%**.
- **1H capex: $80.6B**, or **35.1% of revenue**, up **103%**.
- **Capex incurred but unpaid: $29.1B**, nearly triple the prior-year level. That tells you Q3 cash capex is likely **higher**, not lower.
- Debt more than doubled to **$101.1B**.
- GOOGL issued **$30.5B of common stock** and **$19.1B of convertible preferred** — while reporting record GAAP earnings.
- Buybacks went from **$28.3B** in 1H25 to **zero**.
- Shares outstanding rose **1.2%** in six months.

You call that “pre-funding a build-out.” Fine. But from an equity holder’s perspective, it’s also **negative FCF, dilution, a senior preferred claim, and no buyback bid**. A fortress balance sheet can fund capex, but it does not prevent multiple compression if free cash flow stays negative.

And your “capex plateau” catalyst? You’re asking investors to buy before the evidence. The **$29.1B of accrued capex** says the plateau is not here yet. If Q3 capex prints above **~$48B**, the market will not suddenly celebrate “peak capex.” It will ask why FCF is still negative and why the company is still borrowing and issuing equity. You’re early on the cycle — and early can be wrong for months.

## 2. “25x annualized operating income is not stretched”

That sounds reasonable until you remember that **operating income is not the cash that belongs to shareholders**. GOOGL is spending **35% of revenue on capex**. EV/EBIT flatters a capital-intensive business because it ignores the capital required to generate the EBIT.

Use the numbers that matter:

- Core EPS is roughly **$2.61 per quarter**, or **~$10.4–10.6 annualized**. At **$343.50**, that’s about **33x core earnings**.
- On annualized 1H FCF of roughly **$8.6B**, the market cap is an absurd multiple. Q2 FCF was **negative**.
- The **$124B equity-stake book** you want to credit is marked at fair value. The report explicitly flags **$85.7B of cumulative upward marks** and a **$22.8B deferred tax liability** — these are non-cash, reversible, and can swing GAAP earnings in both directions. You can’t use the equity book as a valuation cushion and ignore that it’s a volatility engine.

So no, GOOGL is not a 25x operating-income bargain when you adjust for capex intensity, taxes, preferred dividends, and dilution. It’s a **33x core-earnings stock with negative Q2 FCF and a paused buyback**. That is not cheap for a mega-cap facing regulatory overhang.

## 3. The operating momentum is decelerating at the margin

You’re leaning hard on **24% revenue growth** and **35% 1H operating margin**. But look at the sequential trend:

- Q1 operating margin: **36.1%**
- Q2 operating margin: **34.0%**
- That’s a **210bp sequential decline**.
- Depreciation is up **43%** as the capex ramp hits the P&L.
- SBC is up **32%**.
- The effective tax rate rose to **19.1%** from ~17%.

You call the margin dip seasonal. Maybe. But with **$80.6B of 1H capex** and **$29.1B of accrued-but-unpaid capex** still to hit, the burden on Q3 margins is real. A 34% handle is not guaranteed to hold. If GOOGL breaks below **~32.5% operating margin**, the market will read it as AI-cost digestion, not operating leverage.

The revenue acceleration is real. But the cash cost of that revenue is rising faster. That is the bear case.

## 4. “The paywall protects monetization”

You’re asking investors to assume the paywall works while a well-capitalized rival is giving a comparable AI agent away for free and reportedly hitting **5 million downloads**.

I don’t need the JPMorgan warning to be a proven fundamental shock. The point is that at **33x core earnings**, GOOGL does not have much room for a narrative that questions AI monetization. The headline was published after the close on October 1. October 2 is the first full session for the market to react. And remember: on October 1, GOOGL fell **1.7%** while the S&P 500 rose **~0.2%** and tech strengthened late. That is idiosyncratic weakness, not macro noise.

You say the Meta story is “unquantified.” True. But unquantified negative narratives can persist. The burden is on the bull to show paid-tier traction, not on the bear to prove the funeral.

## 5. “The EU risk is priced”

You can’t assert that. GOOGL is **14% off its May peak**, yes — but it’s still **+15.5% from the April low** and **+9.1% off the July low**. A 14% drawdown is not a valuation floor. The EU DMA appeal delays compliance, but it also keeps the regulatory tail alive. The orders would give AI companies access to Google’s services and search rivals access to its search data. That strikes directly at the search-data moat you cite as a competitive advantage. Even a negotiated remedy could erode the economics of that moat. The appeal is not a resolution; it’s a delay.

And the long-duration positives — the Georgia nuclear deal and Project Suncatcher — are exactly that: long-duration. The nuclear deal has no disclosed terms, size, or structure. Suncatcher is a demo launch for orbital compute, with the article itself framing the hard part as still ahead. These are innovation-narrative items, not near-term earnings catalysts. In fact, if pursued aggressively, they add to the speculative-capex concern.
