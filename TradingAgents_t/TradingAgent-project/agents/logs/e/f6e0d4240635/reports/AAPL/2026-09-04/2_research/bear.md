## 2. The fundamental strength is real—and largely priced in

You’re right that the trailing numbers are excellent. Through nine months of FY2026, AAPL grew revenue **16.15%**, gross profit **21.74%**, operating income **21.67%**, net income **20.01%**, and diluted EPS **22.42%** to **$6.88**. Q3 gross margin hit a record **50.05%**, and incremental operating margin was **48.7%**. Free cash flow was **$110.2B**, up **52.5%**, and net cash nearly doubled to **$62.2B**.

But the bear case is not “AAPL is a bad company.” The bear case is “the market is already capitalizing record margins and strong growth, while several forward risks are not fully reflected.”

First, **50.05% gross margin is a record, not a sustainable base**. Gross margin expanded **357bp** year over year. COGS grew only **8.6%** against **16.4%** revenue growth—great for now, but that is exactly the kind of spread that invites mean reversion. Component-cost inflation, memory pricing, tariff pass-through, FX, or mix normalization could compress it. At roughly **14.6B diluted shares**, each **100bp** of gross-margin compression is about **$1.1B** of quarterly gross profit, or roughly **$0.06** of EPS. Not catastrophic alone—but at a mid-30s multiple on annualized 9M EPS, the *multiple* can compress far faster than the earnings.

Second, the **tax rate is a structural headwind, not a temporary nuisance**. The effective tax rate rose to **17.58%** from **15.39%** in the nine-month period—a **219bp** increase. Tax expense grew **40.7%** versus pre-tax income growth of **23.2%**. You say AAPL grew EPS 22.4% despite it. True. But if the rate stays at 17.6% or higher, it caps the forward EPS algorithm. Assuming it normalizes into a tailwind is an optimistic assumption, not evidence.

Third, **inventory is the most important unexplained datapoint in the filing**. Inventory rose **94%** to **$11.09B**, with raw materials up **259.9%** to **$7.65B** while finished goods fell **4.1%**. You call it product-ramp pre-positioning. Maybe. But it is also consistent with pre-buying ahead of expected component-cost inflation—which would threaten the 50% gross-margin story—or a demand mismatch. Finished goods are not building; raw materials are. That is a bet on future production, not proof of end-demand. The Q4 balance sheet is a genuine checkpoint, and until it clears, this is uncertainty that should be discounted, not celebrated.

Fourth, the balance-sheet “fortress” is operationally real but **not a valuation backstop**. At **$319.97** and roughly **14.6B shares**, AAPL’s market cap is around **$4.67T**. Net cash of **$62.2B** is only about **1.3%** of that. It funds dividends and buybacks, but it does not protect the equity from a margin, tax, or regulatory de-rating. Annualizing nine-month FCF gives roughly **$147B**, a **~3.1%** FCF yield. That is not deep value; it is a premium price for a company facing visible normalization risks.

Fifth, **R&D is growing twice as fast as revenue**—up **32.5%** versus **16.2%**, now **9.34%** of revenue. You frame that as investment. Fair. But if revenue growth decelerates toward high single digits, that opex growth becomes an operating-margin problem. Management will be reluctant to cut R&D in an AI arms race, which makes the margin downside stickier.

Sixth, **buybacks are not the thesis, but they are doing more work than you admit**. EPS grew **22.4%** while net income grew **20.0%**—about **2.4pp** from share-count reduction. That is not trivial when growth is decelerating. And total shareholder returns fell **10.1%** year over year to **$73.9B**, with buybacks down **12.0%**. Q3 buybacks reaccelerated to **$25.1B**, but at $320 per share they retire fewer shares per dollar than at lower prices. Buybacks are support, not a growth engine.

## 3. Competitive and regulatory weaknesses are concentrated in services

The news flow is not a bearish catastrophe, but it is **modestly negative to neutral**, and the negatives hit the highest-margin part of the story.

The **UK £2bn/$2.7bn collective action** alleges Apple imposed stricter app-tracking/privacy rules on third-party developers than on its own services, favoring Apple’s advertising business. You dismiss it because it is an allegation, not a judgment. But the strategic issue is not the £2bn headline—it is the challenge to **Apple’s control of the App Store platform**, which is central to the services narrative and the 50% gross margin. Regulatory risk can compress a multiple long before it produces a cash outflow.

The **BASF face-authentication patent suit** is unquantified. You say no damages, venue, or relief are specified, so it is not a thesis-breaker. I agree—but unquantified is not zero. Face authentication is core to the iPhone. A patent injunction or licensing cost is a low-probability, non-zero risk, and it belongs in the risk discount.

Then there is **App Store weakness** and the opaque **Jefferies “setback”** headline. You dismiss Jefferies because no target, rating, or numbers were disclosed, and you point to Evercore ISI’s Outperform reiteration. But that is exactly the problem: the analyst signal is mixed and opaque. A single bullish reiteration does not offset a headline saying “a key premium strategy may have just lost momentum.” If that premium strategy is services/pricing, it attacks the core margin thesis. And the foldable-iPhone/AI stories are, as you admit, call options—not current fundamentals. Meanwhile, one of the AI pieces literally frames AAPL as **“late to AI.”** That is a competitive weakness, not a reason to pay a premium multiple.
