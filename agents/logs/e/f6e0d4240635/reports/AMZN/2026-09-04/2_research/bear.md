
Bear Analyst: ## Bear case for AMZN — the bull is winning the operating debate and losing the investment one

Bull, you asked me to bring you a demand problem. I’m going to push back on the premise: **AMZN does not need a demand collapse to be a bad investment here.** A stock can underperform because capital intensity rises, free cash flow stays negative, leverage doubles, earnings quality deteriorates, and the multiple is already full. That is the actual bear case — not a claim that people stopped shopping or AWS stopped growing.

I’ll concede your strongest points up front: Q2 2026 revenue growth of **+19.6%**, operating income **+43.2%**, operating margin **13.7%**, flat inventory, and SBC discipline are genuinely strong. But none of that answers the question an investor has to ask at **$258.51**: *what am I paying for that growth, and what financial risk am I accepting to get it?*

---

### 1. “Negative FCF is a choice” — yes, and it is becoming a committed choice

You frame AMZN’s **TTM FCF of –$11.6B** as discretionary capex. But the trajectory is not subtle:

- Q1 2026 capex: **$44.2B**
- Q2 2026 capex: **$54.2B**
- Q2 annualized: **~$217B**
- Capex/revenue: **27.0%**, up from 19.2% a year ago
- TTM capex: **$173.0B**, +60.7% y/y
- TTM FCF: **–$11.6B**, versus **+$13.5B** a year earlier — a **$25.1B swing**

You say “if capex merely flattens, FCF inflects positive fast.” Sure. But Q1 to Q2 capex did the opposite of flattening — it rose **$10B sequentially**. And the contractual obligations are **$650B**, with **$78.1B due within twelve months**. Those are not pre-sold customer commitments; they are commitments to pay for power, chips, data-center capacity, and leases. You are spinning a **cost obligation** as if it were revenue backlog. The report says contract liabilities were flat at roughly **$24.9B** — that is not evidence of a surging pre-sold AI backlog. It is evidence that the spending commitment is real while the demand proof remains forward-looking.

And the capex has a delayed bill: D&A rose **+31.3% y/y in Q2** and **+32% in H1**. Capex is **2.3x TTM D&A**. Even if capex stops growing tomorrow, depreciation keeps climbing and margin pressure follows. That is why I don’t accept “it’s just investment” as a free pass for negative FCF. The investment has a P&L tail.

---

### 2. The balance sheet is no longer a fortress — it is becoming the funding source

You cite **net cash of +$30.4B ex-operating leases** and say net debt is less than five months of OCF. But look at the direction:

- Gross debt: **$68.8B → $132.2B** in six months, **+92%**
- TTM long-term debt issuance: **$81.9B**
- Net cash ex-operating leases: **$51.5B → $30.4B**
- Net debt including operating leases: **$37.8B → $65.9B**
- Current ratio: **1.03x**
- Quick ratio: **0.875x**
- Q2 interest expense: **$1.314B**, **+155% y/y**; AMZN flipped to **net interest expense** in Q2 for the first time in the modern era

You say interest is under 5% of operating income. True — today. But debt doubled in six months and interest expense more than doubled. That is the definition of a deteriorating trajectory. “Less than five months of OCF” is not a fortress when **$78.1B of contractual obligations are due within a year** and capex is running at a **$217B annualized pace**. The balance sheet is now plugging a negative-FCF gap. That is a credit risk and an equity risk, even if it is not an immediate solvency risk.

And the cash-tax shield? You cite **$6.6B TTM cash taxes versus a $27.8B book tax bill** as a bull point. I see a **deferral**, not a gift. H1 2026 had **$30.5B of deferred tax expense**. When accelerated depreciation on the AI buildout normalizes, cash taxes move toward the book rate — a multi-billion-dollar annual cash headwind. You cannot simultaneously celebrate the shield and ignore its expiration.

---

### 3. The earnings-quality problem is a multiple trap, not a footnote

You concede the **$62.8B H1 pre-tax mark-up** and the **$50.5B Q2 mark-up**, then dismiss it because core EPS still grew ~29%. Fine — but the market does not average the two. Reported Q2 EPS was **$5.75**; core EPS was roughly **$2.16**. TTM reported EPS is about **$12.42**; core TTM EPS is roughly **$8.0**. At **$258.51**, AMZN is around **32x core TTM earnings**, not the roughly **21x** the GAAP number implies.

That is the bear case in one sentence: **the screen says cheap; the business says expensive.** And the **$122.3B non-marketable equity portfolio** — 11.2% of total assets — is marked through the P&L, has no readily determinable fair value, and is illiquid. You call it latent AI value. I call it mark-to-model risk that inflates both EPS and book value. If those marks reverse, the “quality” story weakens fast.

Your Q2 2027 “air pocket” dismissal is too casual. If the mark-up does not repeat, GAAP EPS could fall roughly **60% y/y** on a like-for-like basis despite strong operations. You say by then the market will value the operating line. Maybe. But that is a hope about market behavior, not a fact in the financials. Investors holding through that reset could face a headline EPS shock and multiple compression at the same time.

The DOJ beef-pricing review is similarly modest — Amazon was one of eight retailers contacted, and Walmart’s positive reaction undercuts a simple negative read. Fine.

The bigger issue is the AI-cloud narrative you’re leaning on. The fundamentals extract explicitly says **AWS segment revenue and margin cannot be verified** from the available data. The “Amazon vs. Microsoft” article you cite is an opinion piece, not a segment disclosure. You’re asking investors to pay a premium for AWS acceleration while the hard data to verify AWS margin is absent. Meanwhile the sector is in an AI capex arms race — the same article stream cites massive obligations at Alphabet and Meta. That is not a unique Amazon moat; it is an industry-wide spending cycle with uncertain returns. If AI demand disappoints, **$650B of contractual obligations** becomes a fixed-cost problem, not “pre-sold demand.”
