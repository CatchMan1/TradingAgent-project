### 1) “Operating leverage is real.” Yes — and the cash flow is still negative.

You’re right that Q2 revenue grew **19.6%** to **$200.6B**, operating income rose **43.2%** to **$27.5B**, and operating margin hit **13.69%**, up **226 bps**. That is genuine operating leverage. I’m not disputing the P&L.

But here’s the problem: **TTM free cash flow is −$11.6B**, versus **+$13.5B** in the prior TTM. H1 FCF was **−$27.0B**. Q2 FCF was **−$8.8B**. TTM capex is **$173.0B** against **$161.4B** of operating cash flow. You say OCF covers 93% of capex — fine, but that is TTM. In H1, OCF of **$71.4B** covered only about **73%** of **$98.4B** of capex. The gap is being financed with debt, not self-funded cash generation.

And that OCF number is not as clean as you imply. H1 included **$30.5B** of deferred tax expense, while cash taxes paid were only **$4.0B**. TTM cash taxes were just **$6.6B** on **$135.3B** of net income. That is an unusually low cash tax rate — a tailwind that can normalize. If it does, OCF coverage of capex gets worse, not better.

So when you say “bring the negative FCF argument,” I’ll bring it: **AMZN is spending more than it generates, and the difference is being borrowed.** That is not automatically bearish, but it means the bull case depends on the ROI of this capex cycle being proven. It hasn’t been proven yet.

### 2) The balance sheet is liquid — but the direction of travel is deteriorating.

You point to **$176.1B** of cash and marketable securities and **+$29.7B** of net cash excluding operating leases. Fair. AMZN is not distressed.

But look at the trend: total debt plus finance leases rose **80.5% in six months**, from **$81.1B** to **$146.4B**. H1 net new debt was roughly **$64B**. Including operating leases, AMZN has **−$66.6B** of net obligations. The current ratio is **1.033**; the quick ratio is **0.875**. Interest expense rose **155% YoY** in Q2 to **$1.31B**. Coverage is still strong at **20.9x**, but the trajectory matters.

And then there are the **$650.0B** of contractual obligations, with **$427.5B beyond year four**. You call that “visibility.” I call it fixed-cost risk. If AI/cloud demand normalizes, AMZN doesn’t get to flex those commitments down quickly. The Generac deal — **$8B headline**, **$2.4B of initial deliveries expected in 2027–2028**, plus warrants — is a good example: it’s strategically sensible for data-center power, but it also confirms that AMZN must spend heavily on power infrastructure just to keep the build-out running. That is a cost of staying in the game, not a free moat.

### 3) “Use core EPS.” Fine — then use the core multiple.

You’re right that headline EPS is distorted. Q2 reported EPS was **$5.75**, but after stripping out the **$50.5B** Q2 non-marketable equity revaluation, core EPS was about **$2.16**. H1 reported EPS was **$8.53**, core about **$4.09**. TTM headline EPS is roughly **$12.42**, core roughly **$7.98**.

So let’s use your number: **$7.98 core TTM EPS**. At **$251.19**, AMZN trades at roughly **31.5x core TTM earnings**. That is not outrageous for a high-quality compounder, but it is not cheap for a company with negative FCF, rising debt, a depreciation cliff, and a capex bill running at **25.8% of H1 revenue** — and **27.0% in Q2 alone**.

And the “hidden asset” argument cuts both ways. The **$122.3B** non-marketable equity portfolio is up from **$16.2B** at year-end. It is illiquid and marked to model. A 20% markdown would be roughly **$24.5B pre-tax**, or about **$2.25 per share**, and would flow straight through headline EPS. That is not a source of cash for capex; it is a source of earnings volatility. You can call it embedded value, but the market will treat it as a wildcard.
