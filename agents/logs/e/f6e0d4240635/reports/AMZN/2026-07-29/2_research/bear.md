So D&A is running at less than **half** the capex run-rate, and you’re telling me the wave is behind us? The $151B of compute and data centers is *still being built*. That is a **spread that widens before it narrows**, and you’re extrapolating the one quarter *before* the wave as proof the wave won’t come.

## 2. The “contracted revenue backlog” — this is the error that unravels your whole section 2

Here is the line that cannot stand:

> “I’m looking at what the money bought: $569.3B in contractual obligations… I call it a contracted revenue backlog.”

**No. The fundamentals report is explicit:**

> “$57.1B due within 12 months — largely AWS compute/power/chip purchase commitments; **it is both the bull case (contracted AI demand) and the bear case (fixed cost rigidity if AI demand disappoints).**”

Those $569.3B are **Amazon’s obligations to pay suppliers** — power purchase agreements, chip commitments, compute contracts. That is a **cost liability on a fixed schedule**, not a revenue backlog customers owe Amazon. You took the single most damning line item on the balance sheet — $57.1B of *committed outflows* due inside twelve months — and relabeled it as demand visibility.

This isn’t a nuance. It’s the whole argument. You said “this isn’t Amazon lighting money on fire and hoping — customers have already signed contracts.” The dataset says the opposite: Amazon has signed contracts **to pay**. The revenue side is the thing that has to show up. That’s precisely the asymmetry that makes fixed-cost rigidity dangerous — you’ve committed the spend, and now demand has to validate it *after* the fact.

And the lever you’re proud of — $53.4B of new long-term debt in one quarter — makes this worse, not better. You’re terming out debt to fund fixed purchase commitments whose revenue payoff is unproven. That’s not a treasury department “doing exactly what it should.” That’s leverage applied to a bet that has to clear.

## 3. FCF — “it’s a choice” is the tell that you can’t unwind it

You want me to look at the components separately: OCF $148.5B, up 30%. Great. But FCF = OCF − capex, and the *trajectory* of the subtraction is what matters:

- FY2024 FCF: **+$32.9B**
- FY2025 FCF: **+$7.7B**
- TTM FCF: **−$2.5B**
- Q1 2026 FCF alone: **−$18.2B**

That is not a business whose FCF is “temporarily” negative. That is a **three-year acceleration in the wrong direction**, and the driver — capex — is growing *faster* than OCF:

- OCF: $115.9B (FY24) → $148.5B TTM = **+28%**
- Capex: $83.0B (FY24) → $151.0B TTM = **+82%**

You can’t simultaneously argue “capex is a discretionary choice we can dial back” *and* “it’s matched against $569B of committed multi-year obligations.” Those are contradictory. If it’s committed and contracted, it’s **not discretionary** — it’s a fixed cost you have to fund whether or not the revenue materializes on schedule. That’s the rigidity I flagged, and your own bull framing confirms it.

And one thing you skipped entirely: **capex incurred but not yet paid rose to $27.0B from $16.8B.** Cash capex *understates* the true accrued capex by a widening margin. Real FCF is worse than the reported −$2.5B.
