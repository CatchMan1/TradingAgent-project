## 1. The "226 basis points of operating leverage" is not operating leverage. It's three cost cuts.

The bull's centerpiece is the margin expansion. Fine. Let's decompose it, because he didn't.

| Q2-2026 line | % of revenue | Q2-2025 % | Δ (bp) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross margin | 52.26% | 51.83% | **+43** |
| SBC | 3.01% | 3.90% | **−89** |
| Marketing | 5.83% | 6.81% | **−98** |
| G&A | 1.39% | 1.77% | **−38** |
| D&A | 9.97% | 9.08% | **+89** |
| Operating lease cost | 2.05% | 2.04% | **+1** |

Add up the three discretionary cuts: **−225 basis points.** Your entire operating margin expansion was **+226 basis points.**

That is the whole story. The margin did not expand because revenue scaled over a fixed cost base. It expanded because AMZN **shrank SBC 7.6% in absolute dollars, cut marketing as a share of revenue by ~100bp, and cut G&A by 38bp** — and then gave roughly 89bp of it straight back to depreciation.

So what's the problem with that? Two things.

**First, it has a floor.** SBC at 3.01% of revenue is already near the bottom of the mega-cap tech range — that is the bull's *own* data point, and it means the cut is largely spent. Marketing at 5.83% is thin for a company fighting Temu, Shein, and Walmart on price in retail and Microsoft and Google in cloud. These are not permanent, structural advantages. They are a quarter where management pulled levers.

**Second, and worse: the D&A line is the one going the wrong way.** It grew **+31.3% YoY** — faster than revenue — and it's only going to accelerate. Which brings me to the bull's biggest error.

## 2. The bull has the depreciation sign backwards

Here is the exact quote: *"Depreciation lagging capex by that much means the earnings power of this buildout hasn't even begun to be reflected yet."*

That is precisely wrong, and it's the most important analytical mistake in his entire argument.

Capex is **2.71x D&A** in Q2. What does that mean mechanically? It means AMZN is **booking revenue from assets whose full cost has not yet hit the income statement.** The capacity is live and generating the 19.6% growth *today*. The depreciation on $98.4B of H1 capex arrives over the **next four to six years.**

Revenue is in. Cost is out. **That flatters current margins — it does not understate them.**

Let me put a number on it. H1 capex was $98.4B. H1 D&A was $38.9B. If the installed base were depreciated at a rate even approaching the current spend pace — say D&A eventually running near half of annualized capex rather than a quarter of it — you'd be adding roughly **$10B per half** to the cost line. Against H1 revenue of $382B, that is **~2.6 percentage points of margin.** Your 13.43% H1 operating margin becomes ~10.8%. On the same revenue. Same business.

And this is before we ask whether Amazon's server depreciation schedule is aggressive. If the useful economic life of AI compute is 3–4 years rather than the 5–6 years AMZN uses, the understatement is larger and nobody outside the company can verify it.

The bull says "the bear wants to charge AMZN today for costs that arrive tomorrow." No. **The bear wants to charge AMZN tomorrow for costs it is deferring today,** which is the only honest way to read a capex/D&A ratio of 2.71x.

## 3. The $650B "contracted demand" claim is a direction inversion — and it's the load-bearing error

This is where I need you to pay attention, because the bull's entire justification for the cash burn rests on one sentence:

> *"You don't sign a $387B tail on a hunch. You sign it because customers have committed to buying."*

**That is not what contractual obligations are.**

In a 10-Q, "contractual obligations" are **AMZN's obligations to pay** — its own purchase commitments for goods and services. The $650B stack with a ~$387B "thereafter" tail is Amazon committing to **buy** compute, power, content, and infrastructure over multiple years. It is a **fixed-cost liability**, not a customer backlog.

Customer backlog is a completely different disclosure. Amazon **does not publish an AWS backlog number at all.** Microsoft discloses its commercial remaining performance obligation; Amazon does not. The bull has taken Amazon's accounts-payable-in-futurity and relabeled it as customer demand.

Read the bull's own sentence back and flip the subject: **"You don't sign a $387B tail on a hunch — you sign it because Amazon has committed to paying."** That is the correct reading, and it's the bear reading. This is leverage on the AI thesis, not evidence of it.

And notice what that reframing does to the whole argument. The bull's case is: capex is safe because demand is contracted. But there is **no contracted demand disclosure anywhere in this dataset.** There is only a cost commitment and an unverified weekend headline that AMZN "tripled Nvidia GPU orders" — which, if true, means the company is committing to **even more** fixed cost at Nvidia's pricing.

## 5. The FCF number is worse than reported — and the bull never mentions why

The bull wants you to focus on OCF growth (+44.2%). Fine. Now look at what's propping up that OCF:

The corroborating disclosure is right there in the cash flow statement: **deferred tax expense of $41.4B TTM.** That is the measured gap between the tax expense recognized in earnings and the tax actually paid in cash.

So run the normalization. TTM FCF is **−$11.6B**. Add back the deferred tax that will eventually be paid: **roughly −$53B on a cash-tax-normalized basis.** Even at a conservative 15% cash rate rather than the 23% book rate, you're looking at **−$35B to −$40B.**

**AMZN's free cash flow is negative *while enjoying a 3.3% cash tax rate.** That is the single most important number in this entire debate, and it appears nowhere in the bull's write-up.

Now the financing side, which the bull frames as a "fortress":

- H1 OCF $71.4B − capex $98.4B − acquisitions $39.8B = **−$66.8B**
- Net new debt raised in H1: **+$64.1B** ($82.4B gross)
- Cash + securities rose $43.5B over the same period

Read that again. They borrowed $64B net, and **their cash balance went up $43.5B.** Why does a company with $176B of liquid assets pre-fund at that scale? Because the spend is committed, the free cash flow isn't there, and the financing window is being used while it's open. That is not a fortress. That is a company that knows the burn continues.

And **the $96.3B of operating lease liabilities** — real, contracted, fixed obligations on warehouses and data centers — are the item the bull excluded to arrive at "$43.9B net cash." Include all leases and AMZN is **$65.9B net debt.** Excluding leases from a warehouse and data-center operator is not conservatism; it's a choice.

Also note what the denominator of his "12.1% debt-to-assets" is made of: assets grew 33.9% in six months, and **$106.1B of that was model-valued, unlisted private equity marks** plus $38.1B of AOCI.
