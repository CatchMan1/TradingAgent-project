**Recommendation**: Underweight

**Rationale**: Let me walk through how I scored this, because it's closer than the bear's tone suggests but not close where it counts.

The bull's genuinely strong ground first, because I don't want to lose it: AMZN accelerated revenue from +16.6% to +19.6% YoY on an ~$800B annualized base while expanding operating margin 226bp and growing operating income 43%. Even after stripping the entire $50.5B equity mark-up, core pre-tax income still grew ~46% and core EPS ~29%. That is a real, verifiable operating engine, and the bear never actually disputes it. The balance sheet is also not a live problem — $176B of liquid assets, ~21x interest coverage, 12.1% debt-to-assets, and even the bear's own stricter measure leaves ~$66B of net debt against ~$27.5B of quarterly operating income. Anyone calling this a solvency story is overreaching.

But the bull's case was built to justify *accumulating here*, and that case rests on two load-bearing pillars that the bear removed:

First, the "contracted demand" claim. The bull's entire defense of a $98.4B half-year capex program and $650B of obligations was "you don't sign a $387B tail on a hunch — customers have committed to buying." The bear is right that this inverts the disclosure: contractual obligations are Amazon's commitments to *pay*, a fixed-cost liability, and there is no AWS backlog number anywhere in this dataset — Amazon doesn't publish one, while Microsoft does. I searched the record for a customer-commitment disclosure and there isn't one. That is a data gap, and the bull filled it with the opposite of what the filing says. This is the single most important reversal in the debate.

Second, the depreciation direction. The bull said capex at 2.71x D&A means the earnings power "hasn't even begun to be reflected." Mechanically that's backwards: the assets are live and booking the 19.6% growth *now*, while depreciation on $98.4B of H1 capex lands over the following years. Revenue is in, cost is out — which flatters current margins rather than understating them. The bear's ~2.6pt-of-margin headwind estimate is an estimate, and I'd treat the exact number loosely, but the direction is not arguable, and D&A is already growing +31.3% YoY, faster than revenue.

Third, the margin-quality decomposition, which the bull simply didn't do. 225bp of the 226bp comes from SBC (−89bp), marketing (−98bp), and G&A (−38bp), with D&A giving back 89bp. That's lever-pulling, not fixed-cost absorption. SBC at 3.01% of revenue is near the floor of the mega-cap range — the bull's own data point — so the lever is largely spent, and the marketing cut is being taken while Temu, Shein, Walmart, Microsoft and Google are all escalating.

Fourth, the cash tax point, which the bull never touched: $3.98B of cash taxes on $120.7B of pre-tax income, a 3.3% cash rate against a 23% book rate, with $41.4B of TTM deferred tax. FCF is negative *while* enjoying that subsidy. Whatever exact normalisation you use, the honest read is that headline FCF understates the eventual cash burden, not overstates it.

On the technicals, I'll weigh this more evenly. The bull is right that the primary trend is intact — 200-SMA rising every session, price 8.8% above it, 50-SMA turned up off the July low, drawdown of only ~9.5% against a 2.77% ATR. But three things break the tie against him: price has closed below the 10-EMA in 12 of 13 sessions and below the VWMA ($262.05), meaning the volume-weighted cost of every recent buyer is underwater overhead; the stock has now closed *inside* the 7/30–7/31 gap, so the "$252 confluence shelf" — a 50-SMA built from prints made at $265–$284 — sits inside an untraded vacuum with no real support until $239.82, about 7.7% lower; and the bull's own base-rate claim that compression in a rising-200-SMA regime resolves upward has two counterexamples in this very sample (−18% and −19% drawdowns). And most tellingly, the bull's own plan says "do not buy $259–$262" while the stock closed at $259.77. That's a hold-with-an-overlay, not an accumulate.

The bear also flagged the late-October Q3 print as a mechanical risk: Q2's EPS was 62% mark-up and Q1 carried $12.3B, so a quarter without a comparable revaluation prints a headline cliff against a consensus anchored on the last two — with zero operational deterioration, and with a $122.3B self-valued private book that can be marked either way. That's a calendar risk the bull didn't address.

So: the operating franchise is strong and I would not sell it outright — the bear's own prescription is "don't accumulate here, use strength to reduce," which is a trim call, not an exit. But the thesis that justified adding right now has been materially damaged, the technical entry is demonstrably poor, and the near-term risk is asymmetric and calendar-dated. Bear wins on the capex justification, margin quality, FCF/free-cash-tax accounting, and the entry point. Underweight, not Sell.

4) What to monitor, and what would upgrade the rating to Overweight. The bull's re-rating trigger — capex-to-OCF converging from 1.38x toward 1.0x, and capex/D&A rolling down from 2.71x — is the right watch item, and a visible two-quarter trend there is the cleanest path back to Overweight. 5) Event risk around the late-October Q3-2026 print. Reduce to target weight before the print rather than after — the mark-up cliff is a mechanical surprise risk that no operating model captures, and a 10% mark-down on the $122.3B unlisted equity book would be a ~$12B pre-tax charge arriving from nowhere. Do not hold a full-weight position through that date.

6) Position hygiene. With ATR at 2.77% of price and near-symmetric two-way volume at the range highs, use limit orders and scale entries/exits across sessions rather than market orders. Keep the residual position's risk budget consistent with a 2.77% ATR — the $252-to-$239.82 air pocket means realised slippage on a stop inside the gap will be worse than the stated level, so treat $252 as a decision point, not a hard stop.
