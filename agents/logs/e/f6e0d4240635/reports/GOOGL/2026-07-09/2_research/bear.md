
Bear Analyst: **Bull, I’ll concede the franchise — but I won’t concede the investment case at this price and this moment.**

GOOGL is a great company. That’s not the debate. The debate is whether investors should put new money into GOOGL at **$358.89**, below the 50-day, below the 10-EMA, with free cash flow halving and the balance-sheet trend deteriorating at the margin. On that question, the bull case is asking investors to pay today for an AI capex cycle whose cash return is still unproven.

Let me take your pillars one by one.

---

## 1. “Growth and margins are accelerating” — yes, but you’re looking above the cash-flow line

You’re right that GOOGL’s Q1 FY2026 operating story is strong: revenue **+21.8%**, operating income **+29.7%**, operating margin **36.1%**. I’m not disputing that.

But your key line — “they’re spending $35.7B a quarter on capex and still expanding margins” — is the weakest part of the bull case. **Capex does not run through operating margin the way you’re implying.** It’s capitalized. You feel it in free cash flow now and in depreciation later.

And the cash-flow scoreboard is ugly:

- Q1 capex: **$35.7B**, up from **$17.2B** — more than doubled.
- Q1 free cash flow: **$10.1B**, down from **$19.0B** — roughly halved.
- FY2025 FCF: **~$73.3B**, essentially flat for the third straight year while capex jumped from **$52.5B** to **$91.4B**.
- Q1 buybacks: **$0**, versus **$15.1B** a year ago.
- Debt: **+$31.2B** quarter over quarter to **$80.3B**.
- Net financing cash flow: **+$25.1B**, versus **−$20.2B** a year ago.
- Shares outstanding actually ticked **up** slightly, and SBC rose to **$7.2B**.

That is not the profile of a company whose cash engine is strengthening at the margin. That is a company funding a massive buildout externally, pausing buybacks, and letting dilution creep back in. The operating margin expansion is real, but it is **not proof that the AI capex is earning an adequate return**. The proof will show up in FCF and ROIC. Right now, FCF is going the wrong way.

---

## 2. “The $467.6B backlog de-risks the story” — backlog is not free cash flow

The **$467.6B remaining performance obligation** is a legitimate positive. It tells us demand is contracted. But it does not tell us the margin on that revenue, the timing, or the capital required to fulfill it.

And that matters because the bottleneck is exactly where the bear case lives: data centers, power, chips, permitting. The news flow already shows **~$130B of AI data-center projects blocked or delayed** in a quarter. If GOOGL has signed cloud/AI commitments, it still has to build through those constraints. Backlog can become a capex obligation before it becomes a cash return.

So I’ll credit the backlog as demand evidence. I won’t credit it as a reason to ignore **halved Q1 FCF**, rising debt, and a buyback pause.

---

## 3. “The moat is widening” — maybe, but the moat is getting more expensive to defend

The full-stack AI argument is real: silicon, models, data, distribution, cloud. GOOGL has one of the best competitive positions in tech. But “widening moat” and “attractive near-term investment” are not the same thing.

The AI race is a capital arms race. The model layer is at risk of commoditization as open-source improves. You frame “sticker shock” pushing enterprises to open-source AI as a GOOGL tailwind. It cuts both ways: if models commoditize, pricing power across AI can compress even as capex rises. That’s a recipe for revenue growth with weaker incremental returns.

On custom silicon, diversifying toward MediaTek may lower single-vendor risk, but it also signals that the silicon race is expensive and multi-front. It is not a guaranteed margin tailwind — it is a defensive move in a rapidly competitive supply chain.

The moat may protect GOOGL’s franchise. It does not automatically protect GOOGL’s free cash flow during the heaviest capex cycle in its history.

---

## 4. “The balance sheet is a fortress, and Berkshire is the endorsement” — the trend is what matters

GOOGL still has **$126.8B in cash and short-term investments** and roughly **$46.5B of net cash** after debt. That is strong. I’m not calling this a distressed balance sheet.

But the direction of travel is not bullish at the margin. GOOGL just added **$31.2B of debt**, paused buybacks, and saw shares outstanding rise slightly. The “self-funder with $127B liquidity” argument is getting less comfortable when FCF is compressing and the company is leaning on the bond market.

And the Berkshire headline? It’s a supportive data point, but the news report itself flags that it is **headline-only**, from a **20-of-181** sampled news set, with position size and timing unverified. More importantly, the market had that headline in the tape and GOOGL still closed **down 0.84% on July 9**, below the 10-EMA. A structural bid that doesn’t show up in price is not yet a catalyst — it’s a narrative.

---

## 5. “The technicals say healthy consolidation” — I say they say stalling at resistance

Let’s use the actual numbers.

- GOOGL is **below the 10-EMA at $359.62**.
- It is **below the 50-SMA at $372.60**, by about **3.7%**.
- The 50-SMA has flattened near $372–$373 after climbing through June. That is not a rolling breakout; that is a medium-term trend that has cooled.
- MACD is **−1.755**, still below zero. The bullish crossover is early, yes — but the histogram is already **shrinking from 1.83 to 1.48**. The upward thrust is fading.
- RSI recovered from 33 to 54, then faded back to **48.17**. That is neutral, not confirming.
- Price is pinned near the 20-SMA at **$357.84**, with the 10-EMA just overhead. That’s a decision zone, not a launchpad.

The bull says the low-volume pullback is consolidation, not distribution. Fine. But the low-volume *bounce* is not accumulation either. It’s indifference into overhead supply. The stock rallied from **337.39 to 367.03** in about seven sessions, then slipped two sessions back to the 10-EMA. That is a failed short-term push until proven otherwise.

Near-term math is also poor: upside to the 50-SMA at **$372.60** is roughly **3.8%**. Downside to the VWMA at **$351.06** is about **2.2%**, and downside to the June floor at **$330–$337** is **6–8%**. If **$357.8** breaks, the first real support is **$351**, then the June lows. That is not an attractive risk/reward for new money.

You say waiting for a reclaim of $372.60 means buying 4% higher. Correct. But I’d rather miss the first 4% of a confirmed repair than underwrite a capex-driven FCF squeeze on a hope that the “early turn” holds.

---

## 6. The earnings-quality issue is bigger than the bull admits

You’re right that reported EPS of **$5.11** is distorted by roughly **$36.9B** of equity mark-to-market gains. But you then use that same private-equity book as “hidden asset value.” It cuts both ways.

GOOGL carries **$101.3B** of non-marketable equity securities, and cumulative upward fair-value adjustments jumped from **$44.5B to $80.8B** in one quarter. That is a mark-up, not operating cash generation. If private AI/tech valuations reverse, those marks can swing the other way and hit reported earnings and book value.

On core earnings — roughly **$2.6 per quarter**, annualized around **$10.4** — GOOGL at **$358.89** is trading around **34–35x**. That is not a cheap multiple for a mega-cap whose FCF is compressing and whose capex is still ramping. The headline P/E looks cheap only because the headline EPS is inflated.

---

## My bottom line on GOOGL

I’m not calling for a short of a high-quality franchise. I’m saying the **burden of proof is on the bulls**, and they haven’t met it yet.

For GOOGL, my stance is: **avoid new money / underweight at $358.89**. I would wait for either:

1. a **volume-backed reclaim of $372.60** that proves the medium-term correction is over, or  
2. a retest of the **$330–$337 June floor** that stabilizes with better FCF visibility.

Until then, the bear case is simple: **great company, rising capex, halved Q1 FCF, paused buybacks, higher debt, inflated headline EPS, and a stock stalling below its 50-day with fading momentum.** You call that coiling. I call it a stock that needs to prove itself before investors pay up.
